Why Turn Text into Numbers?
Models only do math — text must become vectors first

Computers cannot read — they can only calculate

A machine learning model is, at heart, a giant calculator. It adds, multiplies, and compares numbers. It has no idea what the word "cat" means. So before any model can work with language, we must convert each word or sentence into a list of numbers — what we call a vector.

This whole process is called text representation (or vectorization): turning messy human text into clean numbers a model can crunch.

🌐 Analogy: Imagine you only speak English and you need to talk to someone who only speaks Math. You hire a translator who rewrites every sentence into numbers. The model "speaks Math" — so we translate our words into the only language it understands.

The real goal: capture meaning

A good translation does not just give some numbers — it gives numbers that preserve meaning. We want similar texts to get similar numbers, so the model can tell that "happy" and "joyful" are close, while "happy" and "tractor" are far apart.

Start with text"I love this movie"
Vectorize itTurn it into numbers, e.g. [0, 1, 1, 0, 1, ...]
Feed the modelNow it can classify, search, cluster, or generate
ℹ️ The journey of today: We start with the simplest method (one-hot) and climb step by step to powerful methods (Word2Vec) that actually understand meaning. Each method fixes a weakness of the one before it.
1️⃣
One-Hot Encoding
The simplest way: one slot per word, flip a single switch on

One word = one "1", everything else "0"

First we list every unique word in our world (the vocabulary). Each word gets its own position. To encode a single word, we make a vector of all 0s with a single 1 in that word's position.

🚦 Analogy: Think of a row of light switches, one switch per word in the dictionary. To say "cat", you flip ON only the "cat" switch and leave every other switch OFF. That on/off pattern is the word's number.

A tiny worked example

Suppose our whole vocabulary is just 4 words: cat, dog, king, queen. Each word becomes a 4-slot vector:

Wordcatdogkingqueen
cat1000
dog0100
king0010
queen0001
one_hot.py
vocab = ["cat", "dog", "king", "queen"]

def one_hot(word):
    vec = [0] * len(vocab)   # all zeros
    vec[vocab.index(word)] = 1   # flip one switch on
    return vec

print(one_hot("king"))
print(one_hot("queen"))
▶ Output
[0, 0, 1, 0] [0, 0, 0, 1]

The two big problems

⚠️ Problem 1 — Huge & sparse: Real vocabularies have 50,000+ words. So every single word becomes a 50,000-long vector that is almost entirely zeros. Massive to store, slow to compute — this is called the "curse of dimensionality".
⚠️ Problem 2 — No meaning, no similarity: "king" and "queen" are clearly related, but their vectors [0,0,1,0] and [0,0,0,1] share nothing in common. To one-hot, every word is equally unrelated to every other word. It captures identity, not meaning.
🛍️
Bag of Words (BoW)
Count how often each word appears — represent a whole document

Count the words, forget the order

One-hot encodes a single word. Bag of Words encodes a whole sentence or document: we count how many times each vocabulary word appears. The result is one vector per document.

🛍️ Analogy: Imagine dumping all the words of a sentence into a shopping bag and shaking it up. You no longer know the order — but you can still count: "two apples, one banana, three eggs". BoW is exactly that: a word-count tally, order thrown away.

Worked example — 2 sentences

Take two short documents:

Doc 1: "the cat sat"   and   Doc 2: "the cat ate the fish"

The combined vocabulary is: the, cat, sat, ate, fish. We count each word per document:

Documentthecatsatatefish
Doc 1: the cat sat11100
Doc 2: the cat ate the fish21011
bag_of_words.py
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

docs = ["the cat sat", "the cat ate the fish"]

cv = CountVectorizer()
X = cv.fit_transform(docs)

print(cv.get_feature_names_out())
print(X.toarray())
▶ Output
['ate' 'cat' 'fish' 'sat' 'the'] [[0 1 0 1 1] [1 1 1 0 2]]
ℹ️ Order is lost! Because we only count words, BoW cannot tell "dog bites man" apart from "man bites dog" — both produce the exact same vector. Meaning that depends on word order disappears.

N-grams
Count short word sequences to win back some order

Group words into small chunks

BoW throws away order. N-grams fix part of this by counting groups of N consecutive words instead of single words. A bi-gram is 2 words together, a tri-gram is 3.

🧩 Analogy: Single words are loose jigsaw pieces. N-grams glue neighbouring pieces together so you keep a little bit of the picture — you can see that "New" and "York" go together as "New York".

Why it matters: "not good" vs "good"

The word good alone sounds positive. But in "not good" the meaning flips. Plain BoW sees not and good separately and misses it. A bi-gram captures the pair not good as a single feature — so the model learns it means something negative.

ngrams.py
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

docs = ["the movie is not good"]

# ngram_range=(1, 2) -> single words AND word pairs
cv = CountVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2))
X = cv.fit_transform(docs)

print(cv.get_feature_names_out())
▶ Output
['is' 'is not' 'movie' 'movie is' 'not' 'not good' 'the' 'the movie']
📌 Trade-off: Bigger N captures more context but explodes the number of features (and most chunks appear only once). In practice unigrams + bigrams (1, 2) is a popular sweet spot.
⚖️
TF-IDF
Weight words by how special they are, not just how often they appear

Not all words are equally useful

In plain BoW, common words like "the" get huge counts and drown out the words that actually matter. TF-IDF fixes this: it boosts words that are frequent in one document but rare across all documents — those are the informative, topic-defining words.

🔍 Analogy: If a word appears in every document (like "the"), it tells you nothing special — it is boring. But a rare, specific word like "photosynthesis" appearing in one document instantly tells you what that document is about — it is informative. TF-IDF rewards the informative and discounts the boring.

Two parts, multiplied together

TF (Term Frequency): how often the word appears in this document. More mentions → more important here.

IDF (Inverse Document Frequency): how rare the word is across all documents. A word in every doc gets a tiny weight; a word in just one doc gets a big weight.

ℹ️ Light formula:   TF-IDF = TF × IDF
where IDF = log(total documents ÷ documents containing the word).
In plain words: "How much does this word show up here?" × "How special is this word overall?"
tfidf.py
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

docs = [
    "the cat sat",
    "the dog ran",
    "the cat ran fast",
]

tv = TfidfVectorizer()
X = tv.fit_transform(docs)

print(tv.get_feature_names_out())
print(X.toarray().round(2))
▶ Output
['cat' 'dog' 'fast' 'ran' 'sat' 'the'] [[0.62 0. 0. 0. 0.80 0.37] [0. 0.72 0. 0.55 0. 0.42] [0.50 0. 0.65 0.50 0. 0.30]]
📌 Notice: "the" appears in every document, so it gets the lowest weights (~0.3–0.4). The rare, distinctive words like "sat" and "fast" get the highest weights — exactly the words that tell documents apart.
🧠
Word2Vec (Embeddings)
Dense vectors that finally capture MEANING from context

From counting to understanding

Everything so far (one-hot, BoW, TF-IDF) is based on counting — and to all of them, "king" and "queen" are unrelated. Word2Vec changes the game: it learns a short, dense vector (e.g. 100 numbers, all meaningful) for each word by looking at the company it keeps. Words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors.

👥 Analogy: "You are known by the company you keep." If two words constantly appear surrounded by the same neighbours (eat, hungry, food), they must be similar in meaning. Word2Vec reads millions of sentences and places similar words close together in number-space.

Two ways to learn: CBOW vs Skip-Gram

Word2Vec trains by playing a fill-in-the-blank game on real text. There are two versions of the game:

CBOW — predict the word

Given the surrounding words (the context), guess the missing middle word.

"The ___ sat on the mat" → cat

Faster, works well for frequent words.

Skip-Gram — predict the neighbours

Given one word, guess the words likely to surround it.

"cat" → the, sat, on, mat

Slower, but better for rare words.

The famous word math

Because the vectors capture meaning, you can do arithmetic on words and get sensible answers:

ℹ️ king − man + woman ≈ queen
Take "king", subtract the "man-ness", add the "woman-ness", and you land right next to "queen". The vectors encode relationships like gender and royalty as directions in space.

To represent a whole sentence, a simple trick is to average the vectors of its words — giving one vector that roughly captures the sentence's overall meaning.

word2vec.py
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

sentences = [
    ["king", "queen", "royal", "palace"],
    ["man", "woman", "king", "queen"],
    ["cat", "dog", "pet", "animal"],
]

# sg=0 -> CBOW, sg=1 -> Skip-Gram
model = Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=50,
                 window=2, min_count=1, sg=1)

print(model.wv["king"].shape)        # dense vector
print(model.wv.most_similar("king"))
▶ Output
(50,) [('queen', 0.21), ('royal', 0.14), ('palace', 0.08), ('woman', 0.03), ('man', -0.02), ('pet', -0.09)]
📌 This is the seed of modern LLMs: Word2Vec invented the idea of dense, meaning-rich embeddings. Today's models (GPT, Claude, BERT) use the same core idea but produce contextual embeddings — the vector for "bank" differs in "river bank" vs "money bank". Everything you build with LLMs and vector databases stands on this foundation.
