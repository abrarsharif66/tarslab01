Why Transformers? The Limits of RNNs
What was broken, and what the Transformer fixed

The problem with reading word-by-word

Yesterday we met RNNs and LSTMs. They read a sentence one word at a time, passing a little "memory" forward. That sounds reasonable — but it creates two big problems.

1. They are slow. Because each word depends on the previous step, an RNN cannot process word 5 until it has finished words 1 to 4. There is no way to use a modern GPU's power to do everything at once — it is forced to go in single file.

2. They forget long-range context. By the time the model reaches the end of a long paragraph, the memory of the first few words has faded (the vanishing gradient problem from Day 6). The word at the start barely influences the word at the end.

📞 Analogy — Game of Telephone: An RNN is like a line of people whispering a message ear to ear. By the time it reaches the last person, half the meaning is lost. A Transformer is like a group meeting where everyone hears everyone else speak at the same time — nothing gets garbled, and it is much faster.

The seq2seq bottleneck

The older translation models used an encoder–decoder (seq2seq) design. The encoder read the whole input sentence and squeezed it into ONE fixed-size vector — and the decoder had to rebuild the translation from just that single vector.

⚠️ The bottleneck: Cramming an entire sentence (or paragraph) into one small vector is like summarizing a whole book on a single sticky note. Long inputs lose detail. This single squeezed vector was the choke point that limited quality.

The Transformer's two big ideas

✅ Parallel processing

All words are processed at the SAME time, not one by one. This makes training dramatically faster on GPUs.

✅ Every word sees every word

Through attention, any word can directly look at any other word — no matter how far apart. Long-range context is preserved.

📜 Origin story: The Transformer was introduced in the 2017 Google paper "Attention Is All You Need." The title was a bold claim — you don't need recurrence or convolutions, just attention. It turned out to be true, and it reshaped the entire field of AI.
👀
Attention — The Core Idea
Letting each word decide what else matters

What does "attention" actually mean?

Attention is a simple, powerful idea: for each word, figure out which other words matter, and blend their information together.

It is essentially a weighted average. Each word builds a new, richer version of itself by mixing in pieces of the other words — but it pays MORE attention to the relevant ones and LESS to the irrelevant ones. The "weights" are just numbers that say "how relevant is this word to me right now?"

🔎 Analogy — Reading "it": Read this sentence: "The dog chased the ball because it was fast." What does "it" refer to — the dog or the ball? Your brain instantly looks back and links "it" to "dog". That act of looking back and connecting is exactly what attention does, automatically, for every word.

Why a weighted average is so clever

Instead of one fixed memory passed down a chain (the RNN way), every word gets to reach out directly to the words it cares about and pull in their meaning. A pronoun pulls from its noun. A verb pulls from its subject. An adjective pulls from the thing it describes.

ℹ️ Key insight: Attention turns "memory of the past" into "direct access to everything." Distance no longer matters — word #1 and word #100 are exactly one step apart. This is the single most important idea in modern AI.

Self-Attention — Query, Key & Value
How a word actually finds its relevant neighbours

Three roles every word plays

To compute attention, each word produces three vectors. Don't panic at the names — they map onto a very human idea: searching.

VectorPlain-English meaningSearch analogy
Query (Q)What am I looking for?Your search box text
Key (K)What do I offer / my labelThe title of each web page
Value (V)My actual content to shareThe page's real content
🔍 Analogy — Searching a library: Your Query is what you want ("books about space"). Each book has a Key on its spine (its label). You match your query against every spine to see which fit. Then you read the Value — the actual content — of the books that matched best.

How the matching works (step by step)

Score every pairTake one word's Query and compare it against every other word's Key by taking a dot product. A bigger number means "these two are more relevant to each other."
Scale & softmaxShrink the scores a bit (the ÷√dₖ step) to keep them stable, then run them through softmax so they become positive weights that add up to 1 — like splitting 100% of attention across all words.
Blend the ValuesMultiply each word's Value by its weight and add them all up. The result is a new vector for the word — now enriched with information from the words it cared about most.
ℹ️ The famous formula: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ) · V

QKᵀ = score every Query against every Key (relevance).   ÷√dₖ = scale down by the square root of the vector size so numbers don't blow up.   softmax = turn scores into clean weights that sum to 1.   · V = use those weights to take a blended average of the Values. That's it — the rest is just matrices.

A tiny self-attention sketch

This is illustrative pseudo-PyTorch, not something to run — it shows that the famous formula is just a few lines.

self_attention.py
import torch, math
import torch.nn.functional as F

def self_attention(Q, K, V):
    d_k = Q.size(-1)                 # size of each vector
    scores = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1)  # Q · Kᵀ  → relevance
    scores = scores / math.sqrt(d_k)      # scale by √d_k
    weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)  # weights sum to 1
    return weights @ V                  # blended Values
▶ What you get back
# a new vector per word, enriched with # information from its most relevant neighbours

Multi-Head Attention
Many points of view, combined

One attention is good — several are better

Instead of computing attention just once, the Transformer runs it several times in parallel. Each copy is called a head, and each head has its own Q, K, V projections — so each one learns to focus on a different KIND of relationship.

One head might track grammar (which noun goes with which verb). Another might track meaning (which words are about the same topic). Another might track position (nearby words). Afterwards, all the heads' results are concatenated and mixed together into one final answer.

👥 Analogy — A panel of experts: Imagine asking the same sentence to several specialists at once: a grammar teacher, a topic expert, and a translator. Each notices something different. You then combine all their notes into one rich understanding. That's multi-head attention — many experts, one combined verdict.

Why this matters

Split into headsThe vectors are divided so each head works on a smaller slice — keeping the total computation about the same.
Attend independentlyEach head runs the full Query–Key–Value attention on its own, learning its own specialty.
Concatenate & combineStick all the heads' outputs back together and pass through one more layer to blend them into the final result.
💡 Takeaway: A single head can only capture one type of pattern. Multiple heads let the model understand a sentence from many angles at the same time — which is a big reason Transformers are so capable.
🔢
Positional Encoding
Teaching a parallel model about word order

The price of processing everything at once

Here's a subtle problem. Because the Transformer looks at all words simultaneously, it has no built-in sense of order. To attention, a sentence is just a bag of words — it doesn't know which came first.

⚠️ Why order is everything: Without position information, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical to the model — same words, same bag. But the meaning is completely different! We MUST tell the model the position of each word.

The fix: add a position signal

The solution is elegant: before attention runs, we ADD a small "position signal" to each word's embedding. The original Transformer uses smooth sinusoidal patterns (sine and cosine waves of different frequencies) so that every position gets a unique, recognizable fingerprint — and nearby positions get similar fingerprints.

📖 Analogy — Numbering the pages: Imagine tearing all the pages out of a book and tossing them in a pile. If the pages are numbered, you can always put them back in the right order. Positional encoding numbers the words so that even though they're processed all at once, the model never loses track of who came first.
ℹ️ In short: word_input = word_embedding + position_signal. The meaning of the word and where it sits in the sentence travel together into the attention layers.

Putting It All Together
Encoder, decoder, and one full forward pass

The two building blocks

🟢 Encoder block

Reads and understands the input. Each block:
① Multi-head attention
② Add & Layer-Norm
③ Feed-forward network
④ Add & Layer-Norm

Stack several of these. BERT is made of encoders.

🟠 Decoder block

Generates the output, one token at a time. Each block:
① Masked multi-head attention
② Cross-attention to the encoder
③ Feed-forward network
(each with Add & Layer-Norm)

GPT is made of decoders.

The two helper pieces, in plain words

Layer Normalization — after each step, the numbers can drift very large or very small. Layer-norm gently rescales them back to a stable range so training stays smooth. Think of it as keeping the volume from getting too loud or too quiet.

Feed-Forward Network (FFN) — a small two-layer neural network applied to each word on its own. After attention has mixed information between words, the FFN does some extra "thinking" on each word individually.

Masked attention — in the decoder, when predicting the next word, the model must NOT be allowed to peek at future words (that would be cheating). Masking hides everything after the current position.

⚠️ Why masking matters: A model that's trained to predict the next word while secretly seeing the answer learns nothing useful. Masking forces it to predict using only the words so far — exactly how it works at generation time.

One full forward pass, start to finish

Tokenize & embedBreak the text into tokens and turn each into an embedding vector.
Add positional encodingMix in the position signal so the model knows word order.
Encoder stackRun through multi-head attention → add & layer-norm → feed-forward → add & layer-norm, repeated across several blocks. Now each word "understands" its context.
Decoder stackMasked self-attention (no peeking ahead) → cross-attention that looks back at the encoder's output → feed-forward, all with add & layer-norm.
Final Linear + SoftmaxA final layer turns the decoder's output into a probability for every word in the vocabulary. The highest one becomes the next predicted token.
RepeatAppend the new token and run again to produce the next one — and so on, until the sentence is complete.
🏆 The big picture: This encoder/decoder design with attention is the backbone of GPT, BERT, T5, Claude, and essentially every modern large language model. Master this and you understand the foundation of the entire GenAI revolution.
