What is NLP & The Roadmap
Teaching computers to understand human language

So what exactly is NLP?

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the part of AI that helps computers read, understand, and work with human language — the messy, everyday words we speak and type, not neat numbers or code.

Computers are brilliant with numbers but terrible with words. The word "bank" could mean a river bank or a money bank. "I'm dying to see you" is not literal. NLP is the bridge that turns human language into something a machine can actually process.

🌍 Analogy: Imagine teaching a foreign visitor your language. You don't dump a dictionary on them. You go step by step — first single words, then how to chop sentences into words, then the base meaning of each word, then which words actually matter. NLP teaches a machine your language in exactly these small steps.

Why it matters — NLP is everywhere

Almost every "smart" text feature you use is powered by NLP. A few examples your students already touch every day:

Use caseWhat NLP does
ChatbotsUnderstand your question and reply in plain language
Search enginesFigure out what you really meant, not just the exact words
Sentiment analysisDecide if a review is positive, negative, or neutral
TranslationConvert text from one language to another
Spam filtersSpot junk email from the words it contains
RAG / LLM appsBreak documents into chunks an AI can search and answer from
💡 Why teach this first: Everything in this bootcamp — RAG, embeddings, chatbots — sits on top of these basics. Get the pipeline right and the advanced stuff clicks into place.

The NLP Pipeline — raw text to a working model

Text rarely goes straight into a model. It flows through a cleaning and preparation pipeline first. Here is the journey every piece of text takes:

Raw textMessy input: "The students ARE studying!! 😀 in London."
CleanRemove punctuation, fix casing, strip emojis and extra spaces.
TokenizeSplit the text into pieces (sentences, then words). Section 2.
NormalizeReduce words to a base form (stemming / lemmatization) and drop noise words (stopwords). Sections 3 & 4.
RepresentTurn words into numbers the machine can do math on (next day's topic!).
ModelFeed the numbers into a model to classify, search, translate, or generate.
🔭 Today's focus: We cover the green-field basics — tokenize, normalize, and analyze (POS & NER). Turning words into numbers (representation) is Day 5.

Tokenization — Breaking Text into Pieces
The very first thing every NLP system does

What is tokenization?

Tokenization means splitting text into smaller units called tokens. A token is usually a word, but it can also be a sentence or even a piece of a word.

Why do we need it? A model cannot work on a giant blob of text. It needs bite-sized pieces it can count, compare, and turn into numbers. Tokens are those bites.

🍫 Analogy: You can't eat a whole chocolate bar in one bite — you snap it into squares first. Tokenization snaps a sentence into individual word "squares" the machine can chew on one at a time.

Sentence and word tokenization with NLTK

NLTK is the classic Python library for learning NLP. sent_tokenize splits text into sentences; word_tokenize splits it into words.

tokenize.py
import nltk
from nltk.tokenize import sent_tokenize, word_tokenize

text = "NLP is fun. Machines can learn language!"

# Split into sentences
sentences = sent_tokenize(text)
print(sentences)

# Split into words (note: punctuation becomes its own token)
words = word_tokenize(text)
print(words)
▶ Output
['NLP is fun.', 'Machines can learn language!'] ['NLP', 'is', 'fun', '.', 'Machines', 'can', 'learn', 'language', '!']
📌 Notice: Punctuation like . and ! become their own tokens. Splitting on spaces alone would glue "fun." together — that's why we use a proper tokenizer.

🎮 Tokenizer Playground — type a sentence and watch it split!

Modern tokenizers: subwords & BPE

🤖 LLM connection: Large Language Models like GPT and Claude don't split on whole words. They use subword tokenization (often a method called BPE — Byte Pair Encoding). A rare word like "tokenization" might become two tokens: token + ization. This lets the model handle any word — even ones it has never seen — by combining familiar pieces. It's also why LLM pricing and limits are measured in tokens, not words.

Stemming vs Lemmatization
Reducing words to their base form

Why reduce words at all?

"study", "studies", "studying", and "studied" all carry the same core meaning. To a naive computer they look like 4 totally different words. By reducing them to one base form, we help the machine see they belong together.

🌳 Analogy: Different branches grow from one trunk. "Studies", "studying", and "studied" are branches; "study" is the trunk. Both stemming and lemmatization try to find the trunk — they just use different tools to get there.

The key difference

✂️ Stemming — crude chopping

Cuts off word endings using simple rules. Fast, but the result may not be a real word.

"studies" → "studi"
"running" → "run"
"happily" → "happili"

📖 Lemmatization — dictionary-correct

Uses a dictionary to find the real base word (the "lemma"). Slower, but always a valid word.

"studies" → "study"
"running" → "run"
"better" → "good"

Both in code — PorterStemmer & WordNetLemmatizer

stem_lemma.py
from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer, WordNetLemmatizer

stemmer    = PorterStemmer()
lemmatizer = WordNetLemmatizer()

words = ["studies", "studying", "better"]

for w in words:
    s = stemmer.stem(w)
    l = lemmatizer.lemmatize(w, pos="v")  # pos="v" = treat as verb
    print(w, "-> stem:", s, "| lemma:", l)
▶ Output
studies -> stem: studi | lemma: study studying -> stem: studi | lemma: study better -> stem: better | lemma: better
🧭 When to use which: Use stemming when speed matters and ugly base words are fine (e.g. quick search indexing). Use lemmatization when you need clean, readable, accurate base words (e.g. building a chatbot or analysis report).

Stopwords — Removing the Noise
Filtering out common low-information words

What are stopwords?

Stopwords are super-common words like "the", "is", "a", "and", "of". They appear in almost every sentence, so they carry very little meaning on their own. Removing them lets the model focus on the words that actually matter.

🔍 Analogy: When you skim a news headline, your eyes jump to the important words — "stocks", "crash", "today" — and skip over "the", "of", "a". Removing stopwords teaches the machine to skim the same way, focusing on what counts.

Removing stopwords with NLTK

stopwords.py
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize

stop_words = set(stopwords.words("english"))

text   = "This is a simple example of removing the stopwords"
words  = word_tokenize(text)

# Keep only words that are NOT stopwords
filtered = [w for w in words if w.lower() not in stop_words]

print("Original:", words)
print("Filtered:", filtered)
▶ Output
Original: ['This', 'is', 'a', 'simple', 'example', 'of', 'removing', 'the', 'stopwords'] Filtered: ['simple', 'example', 'removing', 'stopwords']

When NOT to remove stopwords

⚠️ Careful — stopwords aren't always noise!
Sometimes they change the meaning entirely. "To be or not to be" becomes "be be" without stopwords — meaningless! For sentiment analysis, "not good" vs "good" flips the whole sentence, and "not" is a stopword. Modern LLMs keep stopwords because they need full context. Remove stopwords for simple keyword tasks; keep them when meaning and grammar matter.

POS Tagging — Labeling Word Roles
Identifying nouns, verbs, adjectives and more

What is Part-of-Speech tagging?

POS tagging labels every word with its grammatical role: is it a noun, a verb, an adjective, an adverb? This gives the machine a sense of structure — not just what words are there, but what job each one is doing.

📝 Analogy: Remember grammar class — underline the nouns, circle the verbs? POS tagging is the computer doing exactly that homework, automatically, for every word in a sentence.

Why it matters

Knowing word roles helps downstream tasks. The word "book" is a noun in "read a book" but a verb in "book a flight" — POS tags tell them apart. It also feeds lemmatization (which needs to know if a word is a verb or noun) and entity detection.

pos_tag.py
import nltk
from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize

text  = "The quick brown fox runs fast"
words = word_tokenize(text)

# Tag each word with its part of speech
tags = nltk.pos_tag(words)
print(tags)
▶ Output
[('The', 'DT'), ('quick', 'JJ'), ('brown', 'JJ'), ('fox', 'NN'), ('runs', 'VBZ'), ('fast', 'RB')]

Common POS tags to know

TagMeansExample
NNNounfox, city, idea
VBVerb (base form)run, eat, learn
VBZVerb (3rd person, -s)runs, eats
JJAdjectivequick, brown, happy
RBAdverbfast, quickly, very
DTDeterminerthe, a, this
PRPPronounI, you, she, it
INPrepositionin, on, of, with
📌 Tip: You don't need to memorize all tags. Just remember the big four — NN (noun), VB (verb), JJ (adjective), RB (adverb).

Named Entity Recognition (NER)
Finding real-world things in text

What is NER?

Named Entity Recognition finds and labels real-world entities mentioned in text — people, places, organizations, dates, money amounts, and more. It answers: "Who? Where? When? Which company?"

🖍️ Analogy: Picture a teacher with three highlighters reading a paragraph — yellow for every person's name, green for every place, pink for every company. NER is the machine doing that highlighting automatically, then telling you what each highlight is.

Why it matters — real use cases

NER turns unstructured text into structured data you can act on:

Use caseWhat NER pulls out
Resume parsingNames, companies, schools, dates from CVs
Smart searchKnowing "Apple" the company vs the fruit
News monitoringWhich people and organizations are mentioned
Customer supportPulling order numbers, dates, locations from messages

NER with NLTK's ne_chunk

NER needs tokens that are already POS-tagged, then ne_chunk groups them into named entities.

ner.py
import nltk
from nltk import word_tokenize, pos_tag, ne_chunk

text = "Sundar Pichai works at Google in California"

# Step 1: tokenize  ->  Step 2: POS tag  ->  Step 3: chunk entities
words   = word_tokenize(text)
tags    = pos_tag(words)
tree    = ne_chunk(tags)

print(tree)
▶ Output
(S (PERSON Sundar/NNP Pichai/NNP) works/VBZ at/IN (ORGANIZATION Google/NNP) in/IN (GPE California/NNP))
🏷️ Reading the output: NLTK labels Sundar Pichai as a PERSON, Google as an ORGANIZATION, and California as a GPE (Geo-Political Entity, i.e. a place). The plain words like "works" and "at" stay unlabeled because they aren't entities.
🚀 Beyond NLTK: In real projects, teams often use spaCy or transformer models for NER — they're faster and more accurate. NLTK is perfect for learning the concept.
